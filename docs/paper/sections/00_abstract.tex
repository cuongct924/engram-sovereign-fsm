Modular blockchains obtain settlement and data availability (DA) from
independently operated layers. A chain whose block validity requires Bitcoin
settlement receipts and DA receipts inherits the liveness of those layers: when
either stalls, no proposal is valid and the chain halts although its validators
are connected and correct. Dropping the requirement restores progress but
suspends the guarantees it provided. We present \emph{Engram}, a protocol layer
on a lightly modified CometBFT core (an $f{+}1$ round skip, bounded acceptance of timeout messages and the proposal round exposed to the application) that makes this suspension an agreed,
deterministic state transition. A \emph{health certificate} aggregates the
signed peripheral observations of a quorum, carried as vote extensions, so that
every metric stays within the range of the honest observations
(Lemma~\ref{lem:honest-range}); a four-state finite state machine evaluates it.
When Bitcoin, DA, or the peripheral P2P view degrades, the chain keeps producing
blocks, relaxes the receipt requirement of the unhealthy layer, and excludes
withdrawal-marked transactions; it returns to \textsc{Anchored} only after
recovery hysteresis, DA backfill of the degraded interval, and a
validator-verified zero-knowledge recovery proof over all but a bounded unproven tail
of the interval (the prototype also has an optional heuristic that can stand in for
the proof, which the proof-only runs disable). A layered TLA+ specification
passes bounded model checking at the Tendermint-style and FSM layers
(\resFSMStatesM{} distinct states in the largest run), and in each of five ablations the invariant that guards the removed mechanism fails; the refinement of the composed layer to the
abstract consensus object remains open.

On a seven-validator testbed with Bitcoin regtest and Celestia networks, a composite
fault repeated five times left the chain committing blocks through a full
\textsc{Sovereign}$\to$\textsc{Recovering}$\to$\textsc{Anchored} cycle with
complete AppHash agreement. Under a checkpoint pause, a DA outage and both,
Engram kept \resRQTwoEngram{} of its healthy block rate. A strict receipt-gated baseline that reads receipts at each
validator kept \resRQTwoOob{} and resumed 21--74\,s after the layer returned; one that reads them through
vote extensions stopped committing \resInBandHalt\,s after the fault began and did not resume
(three runs per cell, one host). Against seven
vanilla validators the block interval grows by \resOverheadPct{} (95\% interval
\resOverheadCI{}, 20 paired deployments) on one host; on seven machines in three regions at 100\,tx/s it is 1.9 times as long (three deployments per arm), and the composite fault repeated five times there again ended in \textsc{Anchored}.
The price is the detection delay: \resExposure{} withdrawal-marked transactions committed
before the lock (40--125\,s after the fault began on the testbed, and 47--62\,min at mainnet Bitcoin cadence by model and conversion, during which any sender can submit withdrawals) and none after it, which
checks the circuit breaker against Engram's own transition rather than
measuring exclusion independently.
